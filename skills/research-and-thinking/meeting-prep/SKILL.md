# Meeting Prep

A meeting without a stated intention drifts to whoever came most prepared. This skill does the preparing: it turns whatever context exists on the attendees and the history into explicit goals, the questions that serve those goals, and the tension points to navigate — so the user walks in knowing what a good outcome looks like and how to steer toward it.

## When to use this skill

Invoke it when:

- The user says "prep me for this meeting", "I have a meeting with...", "help me get ready for", or names an upcoming meeting that matters.
- A meeting is approaching where context about the attendees, the history, or the goals would change how the user shows up.

Do not invoke it for a routine standup or a meeting with nothing at stake, and do not invoke it to process a meeting that already happened — that is meeting-synthesis. If the goal itself is unclear ("I don't even know what I want out of this"), clarify it first with intention-clarifier, then prep.

## Steps

### Step 1 — Establish the meeting's basics

Confirm what the meeting is, who is attending, when it is, and why it exists. If any of these is missing and the user has notes or files that hold it, read them rather than asking. Completion: you can state the meeting's purpose in one sentence.

### Step 2 — Refresh the context on the attendees and the history

Pull what is knowable: the last interaction with these people, relevant past decisions or open items, and what each attendee is likely to care about. Draw this from the user's existing notes, project files, or prior meeting syntheses where they exist; ask only for what no source holds. Completion: you can name each attendee's likely priorities and any open thread between them and the user.

### Step 3 — Set explicit goals for the meeting

State two or three concrete outcomes the user wants from this meeting, plus the specific information they need to leave with. Derive these from the user's current projects and the reason the meeting exists — a meeting with named goals is the whole point of prepping. Completion: the goals are specific enough that the user could tell afterward whether each was met.

### Step 4 — Draft the questions that advance the goals

For each goal, write the question or two that moves it forward or surfaces the information needed. These are working questions tied to outcomes, not a generic agenda. Order them so the load-bearing ones get asked before the meeting runs out of time.

### Step 5 — Name the likely landmines

Call out the sensitive topics, probable disagreements, and tension points to navigate carefully — and for each, a one-line read on how to handle it. This is the part the user cannot improvise in the room; naming it beforehand is the value.

### Step 6 — List the preparation tasks

List what to do before the meeting: documents to review, data to have ready, decisions to have thought through. Keep it to what actually needs doing, as a short checklist.

## Guardrails

- Never produce prep without explicit, meeting-specific goals; a goalless prep is just an attendee list.
- Never invent attendee priorities or history the sources do not support; where context is unknown, say so and note what to confirm.
- Never let the questions drift into a generic agenda; each ties to a stated goal.
- Never skip the landmines section for a meeting with anything at stake; the unspoken tension is what derails meetings.

## Output contract

A prep brief delivered in the conversation containing: the meeting's purpose in one line; a context refresh on the attendees and open threads; two or three explicit goals plus the information needed; the goal-linked questions in ask-order; the likely landmines each with a handling note; and a short preparation-task checklist. Where context was unavailable, the brief names what to confirm rather than inventing it.

## Verification standard

Do not call it done until: the meeting's purpose is stated in one sentence; every goal is specific enough to be judged met or unmet afterward; every question traces to a goal; the landmines section exists for any meeting with stakes; and anything unknown is flagged to confirm rather than fabricated. The brief is the acceptance test — run it against the user's real next meeting and check the goals are ones they would actually own.
